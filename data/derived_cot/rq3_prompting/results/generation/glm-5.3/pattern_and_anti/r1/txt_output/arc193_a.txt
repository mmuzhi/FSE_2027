=== ANSWER ===
Looking at this problem, the key insight is proving that shortest paths have at most 4 vertices (3 edges):

**Key lemma:** On a shortest path (positive weights ⇒ simple), any two non-consecutive vertices must have *intersecting* intervals (else disjointness gives an edge and a cheaper shortcut). For 5 consecutive path vertices, classifying by parity: evens pairwise intersect (⇒ common point p_A by Helly), odds pairwise intersect (⇒ common point p_B ≠ p_A). Each odd interval avoids p_A, each even avoids p_B, so cross-class disjoint pairs all point one direction, giving the chain R_{u1} < L_{u0} ≤ R_{u3} < L_{u4} ≤ R_{u1} — contradiction. So shortest paths have ≤ 3 edges.

Thus the answer is the min over:
- **1 edge:** s⊥t → W_s+W_t
- **2 edges:** middle m disjoint from both (when s,t overlap, union is contiguous so m is left of both: A = minW(R < min L), or right of both: B = minW(L > max R))
- **3 edges:** s⊥x₁, x₁⊥x₂, x₂⊥t. Side analysis: if both middles are on the same side ("both left" / "both right"), one of them alone already satisfies A or B and is strictly cheaper (weights ≥ 1), so those cases are dominated. Opposite sides: (x₁ left of s, x₂ right of t) is always compatible (auto-disjoint since L_s ≤ R_t when s,t overlap) → C = minW(R<L_s) + minW(L>R_t); symmetrically D = minW(R<L_t) + minW(L>R_s).

All queries answered with prefix/suffix minima arrays: O(N+Q). I verified both samples match exactly (11, 6, -1 and 157, 124, -1, 114, 114).
