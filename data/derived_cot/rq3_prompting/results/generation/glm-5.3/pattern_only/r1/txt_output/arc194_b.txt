=== ANSWER ===
**Approach.** Provable greedy: repeatedly take the maximum value $N$, which must cross every element to its right exactly once and never moves left; hence its swaps happen at positions $pos(N), pos(N)+1, \dots, N-1$, costing exactly $\sum_{k=pos(N)}^{N-1} k$. Removing $N$ leaves the same problem on the remaining permutation (any solution induces a reduced solution whose costs are ≤ the original ones), so
$$OPT(P) = \sum_{k=pos(N)}^{N-1} k + OPT(P\setminus\{N\}).$$
Processing values $v = N, N-1, \dots, 1$, value $v$'s current position is $p_v = pos(v) - \#\{w > v : pos(w) < pos(v)\}$ (each larger value that started left of $v$ has already passed it), contributing $\sum_{k=p_v}^{v-1} k = \binom{v}{2} - \binom{p_v}{2}$. The counts are obtained with a Fenwick tree while scanning $v$ from $N$ down to $1$ (insert positions of processed values, count inserted positions $< pos(v)$). Total $O(N\log N)$.

Verified against all samples (4, 6, 0) and several hand simulations (e.g. $(3,2,1)\to4$, $(2,5,1,4,3)\to13$, $(5,3,1,2,4)\to13$), matching brute-force bubble schedules.
